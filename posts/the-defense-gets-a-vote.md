# Held Out: What the Momentum Field Actually Predicts

*Part 3 of 3. [Part 1](/posts/capitulation-basket/) is the trading strategy; [Part 2](/posts/a-momentum-field-for-soccer/) is the momentum-field model that computes the strategy's one missing input. Part 2 ended with a four-item roadmap — the defender decision model, per-player skill estimation, the multi-goal convolution, and C.6 calibration. This post is the delivery report, and then the reckoning: all four are implemented in the kalshi-basket repo with the tests that pin them, and the assembled model is then validated against the segment of real match data none of its fits ever touched (§5) — where it wins clearly on ball transport, edges the null on survival without yet proving it, and fails at defender motion under its engagement-tempo default, a failure the same section then calibrates away and learns something sharp from. §6 then answers the question hanging over the whole program — does transport skill actually convert into goal-hazard skill at soccer's goal rarity? — in synthetic worlds where the truth is known, and prices the corpus that would prove it on real data. One roadmap item still ends the only way it truthfully could, and that ending is the point of the whole framework.*

---

## 1. The debt list

Part 2 closed with a roadmap, and each item on it was a named hole in the model — not an aspiration, a documented defect:

1. **The defender decision model.** §7 of Part 2 made attacking play a *discrete decision process* — passes as value-steered jumps — and then §8 admitted that the defense still ran on a fixed script: man-mark your assigned attacker, goal-side, on a leash. The attack chooses; the defense obeys. That asymmetry is wrong, and it biases everything the evolved player configurations feed (jump tables, lane races, the game-view PDF).
2. **Per-player skill.** The pass-completion model carries $s_{\text{passer}} \cdot s_{\text{receiver}}$ multipliers — "some players suck at passing or trapping" as a first-class parameter — that have defaulted to $1$ since the day they were written, because nothing estimated them.
3. **The multi-goal convolution.** The fair-value three-vector $\{P(A), P(D), P(B)\}$ was *next-goal-decisive*: it treats the next goal as settling the match. Good late, wrong early, and the post said so explicitly — "the rigorous fix is the full multi-goal convolution, which is on the roadmap, not in v1."
4. **C.6 calibration.** The gate that decides whether any of this may price a dollar. Every output in the pipeline ships stamped `calibrated: false`, and Part 2 was blunt that the *statistical* claim — "these intensities are calibrated goal probabilities" — was not yet earned.

Four debts. Here is how each was paid, in the order they compound — and then the part a delivery report cannot supply on its own credit: §5 takes the assembled model to the 40% of the match that no estimator ever saw and reports what it can actually predict there, baselines, confidence intervals, losses and all.

## 2. The defense gets a vote

The attacker's decision layer chooses **where the ball goes**: a softmax over pass options, each weighted by completion probability and the *value gained* at the target. The defensive mirror, now in `defend.rs`, chooses **what each defender denies**. Every defender holds a discrete option set:

- **Man-mark** — deny the assigned attacker's receiving spot (their lead position, goal-side).
- **Zone** — hold the shape. This is the softmax *anchor*: a fixed utility $\theta_{\text{zone}}$ that any specialised action must beat before a defender abandons position.
- **Lane-block** — interpose on the carrier's most valuable pass option.
- **Press** — close down the carrier and deny the ball itself.
- **Cover** — drop goal-side and deny the space in front of goal. The goalkeeper *is* this action, permanently — goal-defense is the keeper's decision.

The choice policy is the same functional form as the attack's, because the units are the same: **threat denied**, measured on the attack's own value surface $V(x)$ (the Bellman surface from Part 2 §7; positional finishing danger as the surface-free fallback). The utilities are worth writing out, because each one is a small modeling statement:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_{\text{mark}} &= \theta_{\text{mark}} \cdot \tfrac{V(x_{\text{lead}})}{v^*} \cdot R(t_{\text{reach}}), \qquad
u_{\text{zone}} = \theta_{\text{zone}}, \\
u_{\text{lane}} &= \theta_{\text{lane}} \cdot \tfrac{q_r\, c_r\, V(x_r^\star)}{v^*} \cdot R(t_{\text{reach}}) \cdot \kappa_{\text{claim}}, \\
u_{\text{press}} &= \theta_{\text{press}} \cdot \tfrac{V(x_{\text{ball}}) + \tfrac12 \sum_r q_r c_r V(x_r^\star)}{v^*} \cdot R(t_{\text{reach}}) \cdot \kappa_{\text{claim}}, \\
u_{\text{cover}} &= \theta_{\text{cover}} \cdot \tfrac{V(x_{\text{cover}})}{v^*} \cdot \tfrac{R(t_{\text{reach}})}{1 + \text{occupied}} \cdot \kappa_{\text{claim}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The pieces: $R(t) = e^{-(t - t_{\text{react}})/\theta_{\text{replan}}}$ is the **reach discount** — a threat you cannot arrive at in time is not a threat you can deny, and the reaction time is subtracted because it is common to every option (the discount contrasts *chases*, not reflexes). The lane utility is the ball model's own accounting of a lane — $q_r c_r V(x_r^\star)$ is exactly the expected threat a release moves through it, with $q_r$ and $c_r$ the same choice and completion probabilities the transport model jumps along. The press utility carries the on-ball threat *plus half the optioned threat*, because pressing degrades the whole tree behind the ball — the pass model's own pressure terms say precisely this. And the softmax over these utilities, at temperature $\theta_{\text{temp}}$, is the policy.

$\kappa_{\text{claim}}$ is the piece I did not plan and the simulation demanded. Decisions were initially simultaneous and independent, and the very first smoke test produced a defense in which **all six defenders chose cover at once** — each one independently discovering that the box was the most valuable empty space on the pitch, none of them knowing the other five had discovered it too. It is the same stampede the marking assignment solved in Part 2 (naive nearest-marking sent the whole defense to one striker), and it gets the same medicine: decisions are **sequential, nearest-to-ball first**, and every decided target becomes a *claim* that discounts later utilities on the same ground, $\kappa_{\text{claim}} = 1/(1 + \sum_j e^{-d_j^2/2\sigma^2})$. The keeper's anchor is a standing claim, which is why nobody else ever decides to stand in the six-yard box. Coordination failures in multi-agent softmaxes are not exotic; they are the default, and the greedy-sequential fix is three lines.

And this is not a side exhibit — the layer is in the *main instrument* now. The sandbox above is where you isolate the policy; the app is where it lives.

Two findings from building it, both of which I believe more than I expected to:

**Marked receivers are dead lanes.** I spent a while constructing a scenario where lane-blocking would be the obvious choice, and the model kept refusing — because the moment a defender stands goal-side of a runner, the lane race collapses that option's completion $c_r$, which collapses its choice probability $q_r$, which collapses the lane's value $q_r c_r V$. The model was telling me something true: you don't block the lane to a marked man, because *the mark already blocked it*. Lane-blocking earns its place in broken shapes — a runner nobody owns, a recovering defender who can reach the diagonal — which is exactly where real defenses spring offside traps and covering interceptions. Under the prior θ it is correctly the *rare* choice.

**Pressing beats lane-blocking for the on-ball defender because of the option tree, not the ball.** The threat *at* the ball 27 metres out is small. What makes pressing dominant for the nearest defender is the $\tfrac12 \sum q c V$ term — closing down the carrier degrades every downstream option at once. Without that term the model produced defenders who ball-watched from two metres away while "blocking" lanes that ran right through their feet.

The layer is wired into the shared forward player simulation (`simulate.rs` — extracted from the browser-only code and natively tested now), re-planned every $\theta_{\text{replan}}$ seconds, with the acceptance A/B pinned as a unit test: against a shape-pinned defense, the decided defense **closes down the carrier and the interaction model prices it** — pressure at the ball up, clean shot generation down, finishing down. The θ grew from 49 to 56 entries, appended so a fitted 49-value file from the old build stages as a prefix and the new entries keep their defaults. The seven `def_*` weights ship as priors: the decision layer is *fittable by construction* — every weight is a named scalar, the same inverse-game coordinate descent that recovered the marking weight applies — but no defensive-scheme fit has run yet, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

## 3. Names on the skill hooks

The pass model multiplies completion by $s_{\text{passer}} \cdot s_{\text{receiver}}$, clamped to $[0.2, 1.5]$, defaulting to $1$. The estimator that fills them in, `skill.rs`, needed two things: **events** and **identity**. It gets both from tracking alone.

**Events.** A pass is harvested as: an upward crossing of the ball's speed through the kick threshold (the same 9 m/s detector as Part 2's release-rate fit, on raw consecutive step velocities — the glitch-cap lesson from §6 still applies); a flight chased until the speed settles or the possession run dies; and the run structure itself as the outcome label. A run that dies inside the flight window *is* a failed pass — that is what a turnover-by-pass looks like in this export. Passer = nearest in-possession player at release; receiver = nearest at settle. No event feed, no labels bought.

**Identity.** The subtle one. The crate's CSV schema labels players by *role* — team 0 is whoever possesses — and the DFL export flips that per possession run, so "team 0, third player" is not a person. But the export's entity labels (`p0`…`p21`) *are* stable physical ids; the loader just used to throw them away. `frame::Player` now carries a `pid`, and the whole estimation keys on it. Exports without identity yield zero events, loudly, rather than a table of confident nonsense about nobody in particular.

**The estimator** is a shrunk method-of-moments fixed point on the model the transport already uses. With $\hat c_e$ the *model's own* lane-race completion for event $e$ at skills $\equiv 1$:

$$
s_i \;\leftarrow\; \frac{n_0 + \sum_{e \ni i} y_e}{\,n_0 + \sum_{e \ni i} \hat c_e \, s_{\text{other}(e)}\,},
$$

alternated over players until stable — observed completions over expected completions, with $n_0$ pseudo-events of prior mass so a player with no data sits at exactly $1$ and data moves them only as far as it earns. The final table is pinned so the **median** well-sampled player is $1.0$: a mean pin lets one genuinely bad player drag the whole squad's scale (found immediately by the recovery test — the butcher pulled everyone else up to 1.27), and a median doesn't move. Model-level completion bias also cannot leak in: if $\hat c$ is globally miscalibrated, the pin absorbs it instead of the players.

![Skill recovery on scripted tracking](/images/the-defense-gets-a-vote/skills_recovery.svg)
*The recovery test the CLI runs end-to-end: five identified passers, 30 passes each, outcomes scripted (the estimator sees only tracking). The 40%-completion butcher lands lowest, the 95% metronome highest, the three identical mid players cluster near 1. The scale is compressed — shrinkage plus pooled pass/trap roles — and that is the honest resolution: one match of data earns an ordering, not a magnitude.*

`fit --skills skills.json` writes the table; `mu --coupled --skills skills.json` stamps it onto the live state, where it flows into lane races, jump tables, turnover hazards — everywhere completion matters. On thirty passes a player the shrinkage keeps every multiplier close to 1, which is correct: the hook exists so that a *season* of a player's passes means something, not so that one bad half convicts anyone.

## 4. Beyond the next goal

The three-vector from Parts 1–2 is next-goal-decisive: $P(A) \approx \int \lambda_A S$, $P(D) \approx S(T)$, treating the next goal as final. From a tied 85th minute that is nearly true. From the 55th it is badly false — an equaliser conceded is not a loss — and the strategy's own §10 skew inequality was quietly consuming a window-unconditional 18.7% because nothing minute-conditional and *score-state-aware* existed.

The convolution, `convolve.rs`, composes the full terminal law with a structure that matches exactly what the model knows:

- **While no goal has arrived**, goals hazard at the state-conditioned conditional intensities $\lambda_g(s)$ — the coupled propagation's first-goal law, used precisely as computed (those λ's are conditional on no goal yet; that *is* the DP's no-goal branch).
- **After any goal**, the current configuration is dead information — the match restarts from a kickoff we know nothing about — so subsequent goals arrive at the **neutral marginal rates**: the same coupled pipeline run with the configuration-memory weight collapsed to zero. This is not a new assumption; it is the τ_state relaxation the field already commits to, applied at the goal boundary.

Mechanically it is an exact dynamic program on the (score difference, any-goal flag) lattice: mass is conserved to machine precision, the cap is irrelevant to the outcome probabilities (edge mass keeps its sign), and both regime claims are unit tests — agreement with next-goal-decisive within 1% at 88′, material divergence at 55′.

![Multi-goal vs next-goal-decisive across entry minutes](/images/the-defense-gets-a-vote/multigoal_vs_minute.svg)
*The same tied live state and the same conditional intensities, composed both ways, for every entry minute from 45′ to 88′. Next-goal-decisive (dashed) overprices the favoured side by 15.5 points at 45′ — it books the first goal as final and never gives the equaliser its channel — while the draw is symmetrically underpriced (a draw is not just 0–0; it is 1–1 and 2–2). By 88′ the two laws agree to a couple of points, which is why Part 1's late-game math was never wrong to use the simple form.*

What this buys the strategy is concrete. Part 1's §10 subsidised-upside engine prices a tail iff $P(\text{side wins from here}) > p$, and flagged its own input as "asserted, not proven" — window-unconditional, score-blind. The convolution gives that inequality a minute-conditional, score-state-conditional left-hand side *with the correct structure*: from $-1$ down at 70′, from level at 55′, from $+1$ up at 85′, each its own law. Class B and Class C setups — leads and deficits — become priceable objects instead of footnotes. Structure, not yet calibrated truth: the numbers inherit `calibrated: false` from the intensities underneath, and the post-goal rates are score-blind (no parked bus, no desperation surge) until a real corpus exists to fit those effects. The DP is exact; its inputs are still the model's.

## 5. Held out: what the model actually predicts

Sections 2–4 delivered machinery, and machinery is cheap to praise on the data it was built from. The only question that matters is the one none of the above answers: **on match data the model has never seen, does it predict anything better than not having a model?** Here is the study that answers it, run by the new `validate` subcommand. The scorekeeping vocabulary it leans on is supplied just below rather than assumed — it is the same vocabulary §7's gate speaks.

**The protocol.** The shipped DFL match is split *temporally* at a possession-run boundary (a *run* is one maximal stretch of uninterrupted same-team play — the tracking export's atomic sequence, and this study's independence unit): the first 60% (56,643 frames, 1,078 possession runs, through minute ~68) is the training segment, and the last 40% (37,297 frames, 614 runs) is touched by nothing except scoring. Temporal is the operative word. Consecutive tracking frames are 40 ms apart and nearly identical, so a shuffled train/test split would seed the training set with near-copies of every "held-out" moment — the classic [leakage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leakage_(machine_learning)) that makes garbage models look brilliant. Splitting once, in time, at a boundary where play was already interrupted means the evaluation segment shares no moment, no possession, and no fitted statistic with anything the model saw. Everything learnable is fit on train only: θ — the model's vector of ~56 named parameters from Part 2, its drift weights, volatility scales, and turnover/out hazard rates — with the drift fit improving its velocity RMSE from 5.84 to 4.23 m/s over 3,604 samples; the per-player skill table (401 harvested passes); and every constant the baselines lean on. Then, from 900 anchor states strided through the evaluation runs — each a real frozen moment with real velocities — every contender predicts forward and gets scored against what actually happened. Uncertainty comes from a [block bootstrap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bootstrapping_(statistics)#Block_bootstrap) that resamples whole possession runs rather than individual anchors: anchors inside one possession share its fate, and treating them as independent is how you fake significance.

**Scorekeeping, briefly.** Three ideas carry the whole section:

- *Proper scores.* Both metrics below are [proper scoring rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scoring_rule) — scores constructed so that your expected score is maximised by reporting what you actually believe. Hedging doesn't help, bluffing confidence doesn't help; the score is an honesty-compatible currency. The study uses the two standard ones: the **logarithmic score** where a full probability density is on trial ("where will the ball be") and the **[Brier score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brier_score)** where a single probability is on trial ("will the possession survive").
- *Log-likelihood, in nats.* A model's log score at one anchor is $\ln \hat p(\text{what happened})$: the log of the probability it assigned to the outcome that actually occurred. The unit is the [nat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nat_(unit)), and only *differences* mean anything: a model scoring $\Delta$ nats above another put $e^{\Delta}$ times more probability on reality, per anchor, on average — +0.69 nats is exactly "twice the probability." The ignorance floor here is the uniform density over the 805 grid cells (the 105 × 68 m pitch at the propagator's 3 m resolution), $\ln \tfrac{1}{805} = -6.69$ nats; below that is worse than knowing nothing.
- *Dumb-but-strong baselines.* Absolute scores are uninterpretable; [forecast skill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forecast_skill) only exists relative to a reference, and the reference should be the strongest thing that knows nothing you care about. Meteorology settled the vocabulary decades ago: **[persistence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistence_forecasting)** — "tomorrow will be like today"; here, the ball and every player keep their current velocity — and **climatology** — "predict the long-run frequency and ignore today entirely"; here, the training segment's base rates. Persistence deserves particular respect: in pedestrian-trajectory research a bare constant-velocity model [famously embarrassed](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.07933) years of deep-learning motion predictors at short horizons. Physical things mostly keep doing what they were doing; a model earns its keep only on the *changes*.

### Observable 1: where the ball goes

Each model propagates its ball-state density $\Delta t \in \{2, 5, 10\}$ s ahead (conditioned on the possession surviving — survival is scored separately below) and is charged the log score of the 3 m grid cell the ball actually landed in, with an $\varepsilon$-uniform mixture so nobody can score $-\infty$ on a single unpriced cell. Four contenders: the **full Part-3 model** (fitted θ + explicit decision jumps + skills), the **diffuse-moments ablation** (same fitted θ, passes as drift/diffusion moments instead of jumps — the pre-§7 model of Part 2), **prior θ with jumps** (nothing fitted), and **persistence** — a Gaussian at $x + v\,\Delta t$, made as strong as the training data allows with a train-fitted drift correction and per-horizon covariance, so it is really persistence *plus* climatology. Uniform over the pitch is the knows-nothing floor.

![Held-out log-likelihood gain over persistence](/images/the-defense-gets-a-vote/heldout_loglik.svg)
*Log-likelihood gain over persistence on the held-out segment, by horizon. Whiskers are 90% CIs from the run-level bootstrap; win % is the fraction of anchors where the full model outscores persistence. (The absolute numbers behind the bars: full model −3.76/−4.77/−4.56 nats at 2/5/10 s vs persistence −4.17/−5.41/−6.00. The 10 s anchor set is possessions that survived 10 s, hence easier than the 5 s set — comparisons are only ever within a horizon.)*

Three findings, none of which I would have confidently bet on in advance:

- **The field model beats persistence everywhere, and the gap grows with horizon: +0.42 / +0.64 / +1.44 nats per anchor at 2 / 5 / 10 s, every 90% CI clear of zero.** In odds terms: at 10 s the model puts $e^{1.44} \approx 4.2\times$ more probability on the cell the ball actually reached, and wins 91% of anchors. At 2 s persistence is genuinely hard to beat — a moving ball keeps moving — and the model's edge is a real but modest 1.5×. By 10 s persistence has decayed to barely 2× better than knowing nothing (−6.00 vs the uniform −6.69) while the model still carries $e^{2.1} \approx 8\times$ uniform: this is exactly the regime the [Fokker–Planck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fokker%E2%80%93Planck_equation) transport was built for, where "where the ball is going" stops being kinematics and starts being football.
- **The decision jumps are load-bearing.** The moments-only ablation — identical fitted θ, passes smeared into drift and diffusion instead of relocating probability mass along the carrier's discrete pass options — gives back +0.34, +0.36, and +0.51 nats of the full model's edge at the three horizons, and at 2 s its CI includes zero (it can't reliably beat persistence at all). Multimodal mass relocation along the option tree is not a rendering flourish; depending on horizon it is a third to four-fifths of the measured predictive edge.
- **Fitting θ pays from 5 s out, not at 2 s.** Prior θ with jumps matches the fitted model at 2 s (+0.44 vs +0.42 — statistically a tie), then falls behind at 5 s (+0.25, CI spanning zero, vs +0.64) and 10 s (+1.13 vs +1.44). Short-horizon transport is dominated by geometry the priors already encode — players, lanes, momentum. What fitting buys is the *medium-term* physics: how fast possession danger builds and dissipates on this pitch, in this match.

**Verdict: this works.** Not "fails to be disproven" — works. The full model out-forecasts the strongest baseline available at every horizon with confidence intervals clear of zero, its edge compounds precisely where forecasting stops being kinematics, and the ablations attribute the edge to the two things this series actually built: the decision jumps and the fitted physics.

### Observable 2: is the density honest about itself?

Log-likelihood rewards putting probability where reality lands, but it doesn't directly interrogate the *shape* of the claimed uncertainty — and for a model whose eventual job is pricing, the shape is where the money is. A density can be right about the ball's likely direction and still be [miscalibrated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calibration_(statistics)) about how sure it is: **overconfident** (its "sure" regions miss too often — the failure that buys bad trades at confident prices) or **conservative** (its "sure" regions catch more than advertised — inefficient, but the failure mode that merely leaves money on the table).

The instrument: for any $\gamma$, the density's $\gamma$-**highest-density region** is the smallest set of cells holding $\gamma$ of its probability — the model's own "$\gamma$-confidence zone," the density analogue of a [credible interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credible_interval). Calibration is then a promise you can audit by counting: *the 30% zone should catch the ball 30% of the time; the 90% zone, 90%.* Do that across every anchor and every $\gamma$ (this is the [probability-integral-transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_integral_transform) trick, discretised) and you get the coverage curves below: nominal promise on the x-axis, realised catch-rate on the y-axis, and an honest density hugging the diagonal.

![Held-out HDR coverage curves](/images/the-defense-gets-a-vote/heldout_coverage.svg)
*Coverage curves for the full model (greens, one per horizon) and persistence (grey dashed). Diagonal = perfectly calibrated; above = conservative; below = overconfident.*

**This figure is good news for the model.** A calibration audit has two failure modes, and they are not symmetric. **Overconfidence** — your 90% zones deliver less than 90% — is the dangerous one; it is the lie that buys bad trades at confident prices. **Vagueness** — passing the audit by spreading probability over half the pitch — is the useless one; any forecast can keep promises it barely makes. The model avoids the dangerous failure almost everywhere and never resorts to the useless one. Persistence survives *only* by the useless one. The curves fill in the detail:

1. **The model keeps its big promises.** Its 90% zones deliver 93%, 82%, 93% at 2, 5, 10 s. Two kept outright; one 8-point shortfall at 5 s — roughly one ball in five lands outside the 5 s "90%" zone, mostly long clearances and switches that outrun the spread. That shortfall is the *single* negative fact in this figure. It is also mechanically fixable (widen the 5 s density a touch, pay a little sharpness), which is why it is a blemish and not a wound.
2. **The model over-delivers on its small promises, which means it is better than it claims.** Its 10% zone catches ~33% of realised positions at every horizon; its 50% zone catches 74–80%. Read that plainly: when the model points at its most-likely sliver of pitch, the ball is there three times as often as the model is willing to promise. Strictly, that is miscalibration too — the density hedges, on purpose (the $\varepsilon$ floor, the diffusion floors, and the [relax-to-neutral fade](/posts/a-momentum-field-for-soccer/) all widen it, because under-claiming is the survivable direction) — but it is the miscalibration you *want* to be guilty of, and Observable 1 already showed the model wins the forecasting contest even while hedging.
3. **Persistence's higher curves are the weak forecaster's tell, not a rival virtue.** Its "10% zone" at 10 s catches 74% of balls — because its fitted spread there is a ~31 m Gaussian and that "zone" is a third of the pitch. High coverage from a vague density is a promise kept by promising nothing. The un-gameable read is coverage *jointly with* Observable 1's log-likelihood: the model concentrates 8× more probability than uniform on the right cell at 10 s while keeping its promises; persistence's density has collapsed to barely 2× uniform there. It holds one of the two virtues; the model holds both.

**Verdict: positive — the model's stated uncertainty is safe to act on, and it is simultaneously the sharpest forecaster in the field.** Precisely: never vague, over-delivering in the core everywhere, big promises kept at 2 s and 10 s, one named 8-point tail shortfall at 5 s. In short: *when this model tells you where the ball is going, it is at least as sure as it says it is — except one tail, which is disclosed.* Nothing about this is a tie with the null.

### Observable 3: does the possession survive?

Transport was scored only where the run continued; the model's *unconditioned* output also predicts *whether* it continues — the surviving probability mass under the fitted turnover/out hazards is, literally, its probability that the possession is still alive $\Delta t$ from now. (This is also why the table below says $n = 900$ while the transport rows said 514/282/136: survival has a known outcome at *every* anchor — the run either lasted another $\Delta t$ or it didn't — whereas transport can only be scored where it did.)

A predicted probability is scored with the [Brier score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brier_score): the squared gap between the stated probability and what happened, $(\hat p - y)^2$, averaged over anchors. Zero is clairvoyance; saying 50% about everything scores 0.25; and — because it is a proper score — the best *constant* strategy is to state the base rate, which is exactly what the **climatology** baseline does: it answers every anchor with the training segment's continuation frequencies (52% for another 2 s, 25% for 5 s, 8% for 10 s) and ignores the state of play entirely. One wrinkle the table forces into the open: the evaluation segment ran meaningfully longer possessions than the training segment (57 / 31 / 15% realised continuation vs those 52 / 25 / 8% constants — the match's later phase had more sustained play), so climatology also pays for a real between-half drift, which is visible in its 10 s Brier (0.133) sitting well above the ~0.074 it would earn on its home segment. That drift is not an unfairness — the model fit on the same training half and had to survive the same shift; it could adapt state-by-state, the constant could not. Beating climatology is the entire game here: it is how you prove the model's *state-dependence* — pressure at the ball, contested control, pass-lane geometry — carries information about survival that the base rate doesn't have.

| Δt | n | base rate | Brier, model | Brier, climatology | skill | ΔBrier [90% CI] |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 s | 900 | 0.52 | 0.240 | 0.248 | +3.0% | −0.007 [−0.022, +0.007] |
| 5 s | 900 | 0.25 | 0.205 | 0.219 | +6.4% | −0.014 [−0.036, +0.007] |
| 10 s | 900 | 0.08 | 0.122 | 0.133 | +8.2% | −0.011 [−0.026, +0.005] |

The skill column is the standard $1 - \text{Brier}_{\text{model}}/\text{Brier}_{\text{climatology}}$: the model prices survival 3–8% better than the best state-blind constant, improving with horizon, direction consistent everywhere. And every confidence interval straddles zero — the three horizons are correlated looks at the same 900 anchors, not three independent confirmations. Both facts stand, and neither cancels the other: with 614 evaluation runs sharing outcomes, an effect of this size *cannot* clear a 90% interval on one match — the study is underpowered for the effect it sees, which is a statement about the data, not the model.

**Verdict: a consistent edge the data cannot yet certify.** Directionally better everywhere; statistically a tie on one match. The consistent direction and the growth with horizon are what genuine-but-small skill looks like; they are also exactly what lucky noise can look like, and pretending to know which would be the exact sin the gate exists to punish. Distinguishing them takes a corpus — that is §7's entire argument, and §6 below measures precisely how big that corpus has to be.

### Observable 4: the defenders — one decision layer, two executions

The §2 decision layer makes a checkable motion claim: simulate the defenders forward from each anchor — real positions *and* velocities seeded, decisions re-planned on the replan clock — and compare against the realised tracks, with persistence and frozen-in-place as baselines. One distinction matters before any number: the layer has a *decision policy* (what to deny) and an *execution model* (how bodies chase the decided targets), and the execution defaults are **engagement tempo** — θ's 5.5 m/s top speed, hard acceleration, an aggressive per-step velocity bleed — because that is the setting the [sandbox demo](/defender-decisions/) and the game view exist to show: how a defense *would* close down a threat. Run that same configuration as a *forecaster* of ordinary play and it fails hard: **4.67 m mean error at 2 s against persistence's 1.85 m** (worse even than freezing everyone at 3.99 m), and 10.01 m against 6.29 m at 5 s. Real defenders at these horizons mostly keep drifting the way they were drifting; a simulator that dispatches them sprinting at decided targets is answering "what would engagement look like," not "what happens next." It is the constant-velocity lesson from the scorekeeping section, paid in full.

A bad number deserves a diagnosis rather than a shrug, so the study asks the sharper question: is there *any* execution of the same decisions that forecasts well — and if so, what does it look like? Every part of the answer is fit on the training segment only:

- **Refit the execution, keep the decisions.** Grid-search the execution model (top speed, acceleration, velocity retention) plus the shape-holding bias against realised defender motion on train. The optimiser's choice is eloquent: top speed 0.5 m/s, acceleration 0.25 m/s², velocity retention 0.99, zone bias up 15× — *keep your current velocity, hold the shape, drift toward the decided spot at walking pace.* With that execution the same decision layer scores **1.84 m at 2 s (a statistical tie with persistence: Δ −0.01 [−0.03, +0.01]) and 5.76 m at 5 s — now beating raw persistence outright, Δ −0.52 [−0.73, −0.34]**.
- **But look at what it converged to.** A *calibrated persistence* baseline — $x + a\,v\Delta t$ with the gain $a$ least-squares fit on train — lands at $a = 0.84$ at 2 s and $a = 0.55$ at 5 s (real defenders decelerate; raw persistence overshoots) and scores 1.83 m and 5.70 m: as good as or slightly better than the repaired sim. The repair didn't teach the decision layer to out-predict inertia; it taught the simulator to *stop overriding* inertia. The 5 s "win over persistence" is real but it is calibrated deceleration doing the work, and it was available without any decision model at all.
- **So do the decided targets predict anything?** The cleanest test: a least-squares hybrid $\hat x = x + a\,v\Delta t + b\,(\text{sim}_{\text{engaged}} - x)$, where the $b$-feature is the *engagement-tempo* sim endpoint — the decided-target direction, deliberately not a rescaled copy of the velocity. Fit $(a, b)$ on train: $b = +0.03$ at 2 s, $+0.08$ at 5 s — the regression, free to use the decisions, almost entirely declines. Held out, the hybrid is indistinguishable-from-or-worse-than calibrated persistence (+0.002 [−0.000, +0.004] at 2 s; +0.041 [−0.002, +0.073] at 5 s). Slicing to where decisions should matter most makes the picture noisier, not better: on press/lane-block rows the in-sample $b$ jumps to +0.24 and then scores *worse* held out (+0.16 [−0.20, +0.37]) — the signature of a regression memorising noise, though with only 44 evaluation cases that stratum can't detect effects smaller than ~0.3 m, and the 5 s slice is confined to possessions that survived 5 s, i.e. settled play. "No evidence of signal" is the finding; "signal proven absent" would overclaim, and the difference is exactly one corpus wide.

![Held-out defender motion error](/images/the-defense-gets-a-vote/defender_motion_error.svg)
*One decision layer, two executions. Engagement tempo (pink) — the right setting for the threat engine, the wrong question for forecasting — loses to everything; the train-calibrated execution (green) reaches parity by converging to what calibrated persistence (blue) already is. Numbers are metres of mean position error; every fit used the training segment only.*

**Verdict: parity, once the execution is calibrated for forecasting; the decisions themselves add no measurable motion signal on this match.** The defender layer's value claim is unchanged but stays where it was earned: §2's A/B showed it prices threat response into the hazards (pressure up, clean shot generation and finishing down) — an in-sample demonstration on the simulation's own terms, and the natural held-out test (a defenders-off transport ablation, the same trick Observable 1 played on the pass jumps) has not been run yet. As a *motion* model it is now exactly as good as knowing that defenders are heavy and cautious, and no better. What would change that verdict: a corpus (the press stratum needs ~10× the cases to resolve a small real effect), longer structural horizons (line steps and traps that inertia can't see), or decision targets entering a purpose-built motion model as one feature among several. The harness for all three now ships in the crate — `validate` runs the headline comparison; `examples/defender_fit.rs` runs the stratified version.

### So — does it work?

One table:

| question | verdict | the number that decides it |
|---|---|---|
| Where will the ball be in 2–10 s? | **Works.** Beats the strongest baseline everywhere, CIs clear of zero | +0.42 / +0.64 / +1.44 nats; 4.2× the probability on the realised cell at 10 s; wins 91% of anchors |
| Is its uncertainty safe to act on? | **Yes — errs safe, never vague** | 90% zones deliver 93 / 82 / 93% at 2 / 5 / 10 s; the 10% zone catches 33%; persistence "passes" only by vagueness |
| Will the possession survive? | **Directionally better everywhere; statistically a tie on one match** | 3–8% Brier skill over climatology; every 90% CI crosses zero |
| Where will the defenders be? | **Parity, once the execution is calibrated; decisions add nothing yet** | 4.67 → 1.84 m at 2 s under the motion-calibrated execution; decision-signal $b$ ≈ 0.03–0.08 and no held-out gain |

The headline: **the transport physics is real — the model genuinely knows where the ball is going better than kinematics does, increasingly so as the horizon grows, and it is truthful about its own uncertainty while doing it.** The survival edge is probably real and provable only with more matches; the defender layer moves risk, not defenders. And all of it is a claim about *ball physics on one match* — everything specifically about goals, outcomes, and prices remains exactly as ungated as §7 insists.

## 6. From transport to goal hazard: is this the right horse?

§5 ends with a claim about ball physics and leaves one worry unpriced: the product this series actually needs is a **goal probability** — the λ that §7's gate will one day certify — and goals are rare. Roughly one possession in a hundred ends in one. A skeptic can grant every §5 result and still ask: *at that rarity, does knowing where the ball is going move a goal probability enough to matter — or is the momentum field a beautifully validated answer to the wrong question?* There is no corpus to answer that with, but there doesn't need to be yet. This is checkable now, in silico, with the same move §7 uses to validate its judge: build a world where the truth is known by construction, and ask the machinery the exact question. The new `bridge` subcommand runs it.

**The world.** The crate's own synthetic SDE (Part 2's ground-truth generator) produces 2,000 twenty-second possessions. Goals arrive along the *realised* trajectories as a Poisson process with a **known** position-dependent intensity λ*(x) — the crate's shot-rate × finishing surface, scaled so possessions score 1.2% of the time, soccer's real rarity — and a goal ends its possession (this world produced 22). Four forecasters then predict P(goal in the next T seconds) at every §5-style anchor, and every one of them is **handed the true hazard map**, so they differ in exactly one thing — the transport density they integrate it over:

- **climatology** — the training segment's goal frequency; no state at all;
- **persistence transport** — the train-corrected constant-velocity Gaussian from §5;
- **the field model** — fitted θ with decision jumps, trained on the first 60% of possessions exactly as in §5;
- **a true-physics oracle** — Monte-Carlo rollouts of the actual generative SDE, drift, phase and noise all known: the ceiling no learnable model can beat.

**Scored against the truth, not against one dice roll.** Rarity has a methodological trap in it, and the study walked into it once before being rebuilt: with ~1% events, a single realised outcome sample of a few thousand windows is ruled by luck — in one seeded world the evaluation half happened to catch almost no goals and the *true-physics oracle* "lost" to a constant. On real data you are stuck with the one history that happened; in a synthetic world you are not. So every forecaster is charged its **expected** loss against the known true probability p\* of each window — expected Brier $(\hat p - p^*)^2 + p^*(1-p^*)$ and expected log-loss, the [cross-entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-entropy) $-[p^*\ln \hat p + (1-p^*)\ln(1-\hat p)]$, which the truth itself minimises ([Gibbs' inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs%27_inequality)) — deterministic given the world, zero outcome noise, seed-stable. Log-loss leads the readout because rare events live in ratios: at $p \approx 1\%$, Brier differences are microscopic almost by definition (the score is dominated by the irreducible $p(1-p)$ term), while log-loss asks the question a price asks — *how many times too high or too low was your probability?* Outcome randomness re-enters exactly once, where it belongs: in estimating how much *real* data a certification would need, by redrawing goal outcomes hundreds of times on the fixed trajectories and measuring the sampling noise a real corpus would face.

![Transport to goal hazard skill ladder](/images/the-defense-gets-a-vote/bridge_ladder.svg)
*The skill ladder at T = 5 and 10 s: expected-log-loss skill over climatology, with each bar's share of the climatology→oracle gap ("of ceiling") printed inside it. Every bar's Brier edge over climatology is CI-clear at 90% (possession-block bootstrap). The certification line prices the first real-data claim.*

Three findings:

1. **The conversion works.** State-aware transport turns into goal-probability skill *at realistic goal rarity*: +33–34% expected log-loss over climatology at 5 s and +14–19% at 10 s, every excess-error confidence interval clear of zero, and transport alone closing 45–90% of the total skill physics permits. Rarity attenuates the absolute numbers — the Brier edges are a few parts in 10⁵, which is exactly why log-loss is the right lens — but it does not sever the pipe. Where the ball goes measurably moves what a goal is worth. The skeptic's scenario — transport skill washing out before it reaches the goal probability — is false in the one setting where it can be tested exactly.
2. **This world cannot rank the model against persistence, and was never meant to.** Its ground truth is a ball-only drift-and-noise SDE: persistence-with-drift-correction is practically the *true model class* there, while the field model's real weapons — pass-decision jumps, player-state conditioning — have nothing to grip, and it still converts (CI-clear at both horizons, 88% of ceiling at 5 s) while mildly misspecified, which is the robustness you actually want. The ranking question was §5's job, answered on real data: the field model's transport dominates persistence from 5 s out, increasingly with horizon. Chain the two measured results — *the pipe from transport to goal odds carries signal* (here) and *the best transport we own is the field model* (§5) — and the conclusion follows without a leap of faith.
3. **Certifying the first real-data claim costs about twenty matches.** Take the measured expected edge of the model over climatology, add the outcome noise measured by resampling, and ask what a one-sided paired test (95% confidence, 90% power) needs at the real study's anchor density: **≈17 matches at T = 5 s, ≈19 at T = 10 s** (17–26 across horizons and seeds). Not five hundred. Beating the *market* will cost more — markets know far more than climatology — but the gate's first certifiable milestone, "state-dependent goal hazard is real," is a few weeks of tracked football, and now it has a price tag instead of a shrug.

**Verdict: yes — transport is the right horse.** Transport skill converts into goal-hazard skill at soccer's true goal rarity, the conversion survives model misspecification, and the data bill for proving it on real matches is ~20, not ~500. What this study deliberately cannot do is calibrate real-world hazards — it handed every forecaster the true hazard map, a courtesy reality will not extend, and that gap is precisely the corpus work §7 gates. The order of operations is now justified end to end by measurement: transport first (§5: works), conversion second (here: works), corpus third (priced).

## 7. The gate that can say no

The last roadmap item is the one that cannot be finessed. C.6 — the calibration clause of Part 1's spec — says: before the computed λ (the model's per-side goal intensity, the number everything downstream prices from) may price a dollar, it must — out-of-sample, at match level, pre-registered — be reliable, score well on proper rules, and at least match the *de-vigged* market (the bookmaker's implied probabilities with the profit margin removed) on held-out matches. §5 validated *where the ball goes*; none of it validates *goal probabilities against markets* — that demands a corpus of tracked matches with de-vigged quotes and realised outcomes, which does not exist here yet. `calibrate.rs` is the judge for the day it does, implemented so it can actually rule:

- **The corpus format** is one CSV row per evaluated snapshot — `match_id, minute, model three-vector, de-vigged market three-vector, realised outcome`, where a *three-vector* is the {home win, draw, away win} probability triple — and **matches are the independent unit** everywhere: per-match score aggregation, match-level bootstrap. Snapshots within a match share an outcome; treating them as independent is how you fake significance.
- **Reliability** is pooled over all three outcome probabilities into ten bins with an ECE threshold; **proper scores** are multiclass Brier and log-loss, required to beat the corpus base-rate reference (predicting the outcome frequencies is the floor below which no model deserves a verdict).
- **The market benchmark** is an *equivalence test*, and this took actual thought: "at least match the market" cannot mean "bootstrap CI of (model − market) strictly below zero," because a model exactly as good as the market straddles zero half the time and would fail forever. The criterion is that the upper CI limit sit within a practical-equivalence band (0.002 Brier — far below any tradeable edge). Demonstrably not-worse, with the burden of proof on the model, small samples widening the CI and making the bar *harder* — the statistics lean the safe way.
- **A PASS emits a certificate** bound to a hash of the exact corpus it was earned on, and `mu --certificate` is the *only* code path in the repo that flips `calibrated: true`. A FAIL certificate never certifies. There is no argument to a function anywhere that sets the flag by assertion.

A gate with no corpus to rule on could still be a broken gate, so the judge itself goes on trial: `calibrate --selftest` generates synthetic corpora where the correct verdict is known *by construction* and requires the gate to reach it — pass the model that reports true probabilities, fail the overconfident one, fail the noisy one that loses to the market, each failure for the right named reason. To be unmistakable about the two figures below: **they are not the model being validated — §5 was the model.** These are the two ends of the judge's own exam, shown so you can see what a pass and a fail look like on the day the gate rules on real data.

How to read them: a **reliability diagram** takes every probability a model ever stated, buckets them into bins ("all the times it said ~30%"), and plots, per bin, the stated probability (x) against how often the thing actually happened (y), with bubble area showing how many statements landed in the bin. An honest forecaster sits on the diagonal — when it says 30%, it happens 30% of the time. This is the same audit as §5's coverage curves with one dimension boiled away: there the promise was a *region* of the pitch and the audit counted balls inside it; here the promise is a bare number and the audit counts outcomes. ECE — [expected calibration error](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599) — is the bubble-weighted average gap to the diagonal, the whole picture compressed to one number the gate can threshold (≤ 0.05 to pass).

![Judge self-test: the synthetic model that deserves to pass](/images/the-defense-gets-a-vote/reliability_pass.svg)
*Judge on trial, case 1 — a synthetic truth-reporting model. Every well-populated bubble rides the diagonal within sampling noise; the one small red dot on the x-axis is a near-empty bin (a handful of ~0.8 forecasts that happened not to occur — bubble area is bin population, and the count-weighted ECE correctly discounts it). ECE 0.022 → the gate passes it. When a real corpus produces this picture, `calibrated: true` gets earned.*

![Judge self-test: the synthetic model that deserves to fail](/images/the-defense-gets-a-vote/reliability_overconfident.svg)
*Judge on trial, case 2 — the same corpus with the probabilities artificially sharpened toward 0 and 1. Overconfidence shows clearest at the extremes: the lowest bins land above the line (it said ~10%, it happened ~25% of the time) and the highest land below (said ~90%, delivered ~70%), with mid-range bins scattering both sides. ECE 0.167; the gate correctly fails it, naming reliability, the base-rate floor, and the market bar. This rotation is what §5's coverage audit was hunting for in the density — and did not find.*

The takeaway from the pair is narrow and important: **the judge can tell these cases apart, for the stated reasons.** It says nothing yet about the momentum model, whose reliability diagram cannot exist until there is a corpus of matches to draw it from.

And that is the verdict on the real model, the ending Part 2 promised in advance: **there is no verdict, because there is no corpus.** One tracked match validates transport physics — §5 just did, baselines and CIs and all — but it cannot calibrate a goal hazard, and no amount of machinery changes that. So the gate stands built and armed, the certificate file does not exist, every output still says `calibrated: false`, the manual hazard gate keeps its override, and the crossover rule runs on the base rate. The framework's first design principle is that the best output is often *no trade*; the calibration gate's first output is *no verdict*. Both are the system working.
